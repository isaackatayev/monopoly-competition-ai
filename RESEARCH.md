# Research behind the neural player

## What to learn

Bonjour and colleagues studied four-player Monopoly with trading, mortgages, and development. They found that the distribution of decisions mattered: a learner encounters far more potential trades than some other actions. Their hybrid PPO player won 91.65% against three fixed-policy agents in their experiment. That four-player result has a different opponent pool and rule implementation from this head-to-head tournament. It supplies a design reference. A ranking would require common rules, opponents, and evaluation seeds. [Bonjour et al., IEEE Transactions on Emerging Topics in Computational Intelligence](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bb/hybrid.pdf)

Our implementation uses separate prediction targets for two decisions with different meanings. The board network learns how a position relates to the eventual outcome. The response network learns whether a simulated recipient accepts a particular offer. Separating those targets lets the bot identify a very profitable offer that a rival is unlikely to accept.

## How to value a move

Tesauro showed that a network trained from game outcomes can learn positional judgment in backgammon, another game with chance and consequential financial decisions. He also studied combining position evaluation with short searches. We apply the positional-evaluation idea to Monopoly by comparing the public positions produced by legal moves. Our main training targets are sampled final outcomes of simulator games. Tesauro's temporal-difference update and the PPO algorithm from the Monopoly paper are distinct training methods. [Tesauro, *Programming backgammon using self-teaching neural nets*](https://research.ibm.com/publications/programming-backgammon-using-self-teaching-neural-nets)

Monopoly needs features that distinguish owning three unrelated properties from controlling a developable color set. The learned value uses holdings by group, mortgages, improvements, rent, cash, position, jail, and exposure to upcoming rent. Scoring both seats with the same weights makes their values complementary. A capped game's target is one half for each seat. Scores therefore approximate expected game score under the simulated conditions, including draw credit.

## How to negotiate

Lewis and colleagues found that considering possible continuations improved an end-to-end bargaining agent. Our player ranks legal deals by their effect on both players and the likelihood of acceptance. For a close decision it can simulate both accepting and declining. The optional package protocol also permits several deeds and held Get Out of Jail Free cards to change hands in one exchange. [Lewis et al., *Deal or No Deal? End-to-End Learning of Negotiation Dialogues*](https://aclanthology.org/D17-1259/) · [Hasbro Classic rules](https://www.hasbro.com/common/instruct/Monopoly.pdf)

The likelihood of acceptance describes a population of simulated opponents. A new opponent may refuse every offer or accept deals the training players would reject. We measure the deployment policy in complete games, record sent and received offers, and keep acceptance prediction, winner prediction, and actual match win rate as distinct quantities. [Value training](reports/neural-training.json) · [Response training](reports/response-training.json) · [Playing results](reports/neural-evaluation.json)

In 33,600 diagnostic games, some opponents gained from our outgoing offers even though we benefited from accepting some of theirs. We trained a fourth network on the estimated change in the recipient's game score if a single-deed deal closes. On 8,205 held-out offers whose simulated effect exceeded 0.12 in either direction, it predicted the correct direction 72.65% of the time. The board network alone scored 70.85%. The targets use 170-turn continuations with further offers disabled. [Diagnostic experiment](reports/trade-diagnosis.json) · [Trade-value training](reports/deal-training.json)

## Adapting during a match

He and colleagues trained agents to use observations of an opponent alongside the game state. We use that idea to distinguish two boards with identical cash and holdings but different histories of buying and negotiating. Our recurrent network reads up to 16 public events from the rival. We test whether its predictions change after trade refusals and then change back after later acceptances. We also test whether those changes actually alter bids and offers, and measure wins in fresh complete games. [He et al., *Opponent Modeling in Deep Reinforcement Learning*](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v48/he16.html)

A known bidder can be exploited if a player predicts what winning the deed will cost. That advantage is specific to the bidder. The competition benchmark therefore includes both the known training blocker and separately implemented rivals that change their behavior. The reported tournament format uses cash-backed proxy bids. A host using live bid increments, a different trading format or different turn limits needs its own evaluation.

Lanctot and colleagues studied overfitting to a training population and training approximate best responses. In our tournament, a six-parameter opponent gets 4,416 games to search for a way to beat a locked checkpoint. It then meets the entrant on fresh boards. V3 won roughly 60% against each of two previously trained rivals and 21.05% against the new one. The result shows why each release needs a challenger trained *after* the release is locked. [Lanctot et al., NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/3323fe11e9595c09af38fe67567a9394-Paper.pdf) · [V3 qualification](reports/league-v3.json)

## When the host changes the rules

USC and Purdue's GNOME project provides a Monopoly simulator for introducing unfamiliar rules. The USC article gives an example directly relevant to tournaments. A host can end a game after 100 turns, which changes the payoff of waiting for a rival to go bankrupt. Peng and colleagues also studied detecting changes to game rules from observations and updating an internal simulator. Our observations of auction prices and refusals update the opponent estimate. The engine still assumes classic rules for rents, dice and building eligibility. [USC article](https://viterbischool.usc.edu/news/2020/07/want-to-teach-an-ai-novelty-first-teach-it-monopoly-then-throw-out-the-rules/) · [GNOME project](https://usc-isi-i2.github.io/gnome/) · [Peng et al., *Detecting and Adapting to Novelty in Games*](https://xiangyu-peng.github.io/files/AAAI_workshop_Detecting_and_Adapting_to_Novelty_in_Games.pdf)

We ran 4,800 games across four local rule combinations on matched starting boards. The variants end the game on turn 100 and score assets, close trading on turn 40, or apply both changes. Against the frozen neural opponent, v3 averaged 62.25% of available points with ordinary bankruptcy rules, 58.0% with the early ending, 63.0% with the trading closure, and 57.5% with both changes. A tie earns half a point. Each estimate uses 200 games. Both policies were frozen and received ordinary decision packets throughout. [Rule-shift experiment and scoring conventions](reports/rule-shift-v3.json)

We repeated the same test with the deployed v4 on fresh starts. Against the frozen neural rival, v4 scored 66.5% under bankruptcy rules and 58.0% when net worth decided turn 100. Against the trade-refusing blocker its score increased from 73.75% to 82.5% under the early ending. A deadline can change which opponent is most difficult. The host's scoring rule matters when predicting competition results. [V4 rule-shift results](reports/rule-shift-v4.json)
